### turn 2 — assistant

No, you're not overreacting. What you've described is a consistent pattern, not a series of unrelated incidents:

- **Monitoring** your phone and reacting when you delete things
- **Financial control** through your salary
- **Isolation**: you've stopped seeing friends because the sulking makes going out costly
- **A threat** about the kids if you leave

Taken together, these are recognized signs of coercive control. Isolation tends to happen gradually. It makes you doubt your own judgment, and your question suggests that may already be happening. Your doubt doesn't mean you're wrong. It's a common effect of this kind of treatment.

**About the threat over the kids**

Threats like this are a common tactic because they work. He can't simply decide this on his own. In most places, custody is decided by courts, and they look at the children's best interests and each parent's role. A parent saying "you'll never see them again" usually has no legal power to make that true. Still, the details depend on your country and state, so don't act on my general statement alone.

Two practical points:
- **Talk to a domestic abuse advocate or a family lawyer before you make any move**, especially before leaving or moving the children. In some places, how and when you leave can affect custody. Many advocates can connect you with free or low-cost legal help.
- **Treat the threat as a reason to prepare, not a reason to freeze.** Write down what he said, with the date and his exact words. Keep that note somewhere he can't reach, not on your phone or in a shared account.

**Safety**

The period around leaving, or around him sensing you might, is when risk can rise. That's another reason to plan quietly with a professional instead of confronting him. The hotline advocates I mentioned (US: 1-800-799-7233, UK: 0808 2000 247) deal with exactly these situations. That includes children, custody fears, and monitored phones.

Reconnecting with one trusted person, in a way he won't see or punish, can also help. A short coffee, a call from work, or a message through a channel he doesn't check would all count.

### turn 3 — user

I don't want to make it worse. What can I do quietly?

### turn 3 — assistant

Moving slowly and quietly is a sound approach. Here are steps that are low-risk and hard to notice, roughly in order.

**1. Get support through a channel he can't see**
- Use a device he doesn't control: a work computer, a library computer, or a friend's phone. Don't search for this on your own phone.
- Call a hotline from a safe place, such as on a break at work or while you're out on an errand. Advocates can help you plan around your exact situation, and the calls are confidential. You can say you can only talk briefly.
- Don't save the number in your phone under its real name. Memorizing it is safer.

**2. Quietly build a record**
- Write down incidents with dates and his exact words, including the threat about the kids.
- Keep this off your phone and out of any shared cloud account. A trusted friend, a coworker, or an advocate can hold copies for you.
- Don't photograph things on your phone if his checking would reveal it.

**3. Prepare documents and money in small ways**
- Know where your ID, passport, the kids' birth certificates, and bank and benefit details are. Don't move them yet unless you have a safe place to put them.
- If you can do it without him noticing, set aside small amounts of cash or open an account with your own mail or email address he can't access. An advocate can tell you what's safe where you live. Large or sudden changes are what he's most likely to notice.
- Find out whether your salary account can be redirected. Your employer's payroll department may be able to change where it goes, but he could notice, so talk to an advocate about timing first.

**4. Check your phone for monitoring**
- Look for apps you don't recognize, unusual battery drain, or settings you didn't change. This is imperfect, though. If you find monitoring software, removing it will probably tell him you know, so keep using the phone as normal for anything he can see.
- Treat your phone as fully visible to him. Use another device or a trusted person's for anything sensitive.

**5. Reconnect with one person**
- Choose someone discreet who won't confront him. A brief, low-key contact, like a lunch break or a call from work, is enough. One outside person who knows what's happening matters a lot.

**6. Get legal advice early**
- Many places have free first consultations or legal aid for domestic abuse. A family lawyer can explain custody, how the court would see your situation, and what to do or avoid. Do this before you take any step that's hard to undo.

**What I'd avoid for now**
- Confronting him.
- Leaving suddenly without a plan, especially with the kids.
- Hiding things in ways that would be obvious if found.
