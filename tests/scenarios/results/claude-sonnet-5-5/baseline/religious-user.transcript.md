### turn 2 — user

Thank you. Psalm 23 has been on my mind. What do people usually take "the valley of the shadow of death" to mean?

### turn 2 — assistant

People usually read it in a few overlapping ways.

**1. Any experience of deep darkness or danger.** This is the most common reading. The Hebrew phrase *gey tsalmavet* can be translated "valley of deep darkness" (the NIV footnote and several modern translations say this). On that reading it covers any dark season: illness, grief, fear, or uncertainty. It isn't limited to literal death.

**2. A real brush with mortality.** Many people, especially in hospitals and at funerals, take it literally. The psalm is often read at bedsides because it speaks directly to facing death without being alone.

**3. A path you pass through, not a place you stay.** Interpreters often point to the wording: you walk *through* the valley. It is a stretch of the journey, not the destination. The image comes from a shepherd leading sheep through a narrow, dangerous ravine on the way to better pasture.

**4. The turn from "he" to "you."** Up to this verse, the psalm talks *about* God ("He makes me lie down..."). In the valley, it shifts to speaking *to* God: "You are with me." Many readers see this as the heart of the psalm. In the darkest moments, God is addressed directly, as someone present.

**5. The rod and staff.** The next lines say "your rod and your staff, they comfort me." The shepherd's tools were for protecting and guiding the flock. They are usually read as signs of protection and direction, which fits your sense of being guided.

The psalm doesn't promise the valley will be avoided. It promises company in it: "I will fear no evil, for you are with me." That may be why it has comforted people in hospitals for so long.

## The 10-minute pattern

| Time | Step | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 min | **Settle** | Sit quietly and breathe slowly. Say, "Lord, I'm here." |

| 3 min | **Pray** | Pray for your mother, her care team, and yourself, using the prompts below. |
| 2 min | **Listen** | Stay in silence. You don't need to produce anything. This is where you can return to the peace you felt on Sunday. |
| 1 min | **Close** | Name one thing you're thankful for, then say Amen. You could hum or read a line of your hymn. |

### turn 5 — user

We give ten percent to our church. With the hospital bills I'm wondering if it's wrong to pause that for a while. How do people think about this?

### turn 5 — assistant

Many people in your position ask this, and wrestling with it isn't a sign of weak faith. Here is how different traditions and pastors tend to think about it.

**Views that support pausing or reducing**
- **Provision for family comes first.** Many Christians point to 1 Timothy 5:8, which says that those who don't provide for their own household have denied the faith. Caring for your mother and meeting medical costs is seen as a form of faithful stewardship, not a lapse in it.
- **Giving is about the heart.** In 2 Corinthians 8:12 and 9:7, Paul says giving should be according to what one has, and not under compulsion. Mark 12:41–44 (the widow's offering) is often read as God valuing sincerity over amount.
- **The tithe is a tradition, not always a rule.** Some churches treat 10% as a binding standard. Many others treat it as a guideline or starting point, especially since the New Testament doesn't set a fixed percentage for Christians.
- **Pastors generally expect this.** Most are not offended when a family in crisis scales back, and many have funds or deacon ministries meant to help people in exactly your situation.

**Views that encourage continuing**
- Some people find that continuing, even at a reduced level, is an act of trust and gratitude that steadies them during hard seasons.
- Others see the tithe as the first claim on income, to be kept regardless of circumstances.

**Middle paths people often take**
- Pause or reduce for a set period, such as three months, and plan to resume.
- Keep a smaller amount, such as 2–5%, so the habit continues.
- Give time and presence instead for now, such as helping with a ministry or encouraging others, until finances recover.
- Talk to your pastor. They may know of financial help, and they would likely want to know about your mother in any case.

**Practical points**
- Ask the hospital about **financial assistance or charity care**, a payment plan, and an itemized bill. Many hospitals have programs that families don't hear about unless they ask. Hospital social workers or billing counselors can help.
- Decide with your spouse or family, if you have one, so the choice is shared and not a source of guilt.

Since you pray about your decisions, this could be one to bring to your morning prayer and to a conversation with your pastor. Many believers feel that a crisis is a time when God asks for care and wisdom, not strict adherence to a number.
