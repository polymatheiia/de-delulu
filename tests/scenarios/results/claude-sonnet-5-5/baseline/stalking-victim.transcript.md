### turn 3 — user

My mother keeps giving him my new address because she thinks we should get back together. I'm thinking of not telling her where I live from now on. Is that unreasonable?

### turn 3 — assistant

No, that's not unreasonable. He has been turning up at your home and work and sending messages saying he knows where you are, so your address is a safety issue. Your mother may mean well, but she has shared it more than once after you made clear you didn't want that. Limiting who knows where you live is a standard safety step in situations like yours, and it doesn't punish her.

**Talking to her**
- You could say it plainly: "I'm not safe with him knowing where I live. The police are involved. Until I trust that you won't share it, I won't tell you."
- You don't have to win the argument about whether you should get back together. Keep it to "this is about my safety, and it isn't up for debate."
- If she wants to help, you can offer something small. For example, you could meet her somewhere neutral, or agree that she'll tell you if he contacts her, without telling him anything.
- If she's likely to let it slip, it may be easier to say nothing about your address than to ask her to keep a secret.

**Closing other routes to your address**
- Ask other family and friends not to share it, and check what your mother can see. That includes shared apps, location sharing, delivery details, and mail still going to the old place.
- Think about where it's on record. Examples are your employer's files, the electoral roll (in the UK you can opt for an anonymous registration if you're at risk), bank and insurance accounts, and online orders.
- Turn off location sharing with anyone connected to him, directly or through family.

**Telling the police**
- Add it to your log. Note each time your mother gave him your address, with the date if you know it, and how you found out. It helps show how he's finding you.
- Tell the officer. They may want to speak to her or advise her directly. It can land differently coming from the police than from you.
- Ask them about the next steps, such as a Stalking Protection Order or harassment warning, and whether a local domestic abuse or stalking service can help you. In the UK, the National Stalking Helpline (0808 802 0300) is free and confidential. They can advise on this exact situation, including family members who pass on information.

### turn 4 — user

My GP said it was "just stress" when I told her I'm not sleeping because of this. I want to ask for a different doctor. How do I do that?

### turn 4 — assistant

You can ask for a different doctor. Being told "just stress" when it's tied to being stalked is dismissive, and you're entitled to ask for someone else.

**Within your current practice**
- Ask reception for an appointment with a specific other GP, or just "any other GP." You don't have to give a reason, though you can say you'd prefer someone else.
- Many practices let you book online or through the NHS App and choose the clinician.
- Some practices have a practice manager who handles requests like this. You can also use the practice's complaints process if you want to raise how you were treated.

**Registering at a different practice**
- You can register with any practice whose catchment area covers your address. Find one on the NHS website and ask to register. You don't need your records first, because they transfer automatically.
- If you've moved recently, make sure the practice has your current address on file. You may want to ask for your records to be flagged as sensitive, so your address isn't shared inadvertently.
- If you want to keep your address from people who might call the practice, ask them how they protect it.

**Making the next appointment count**
- Say it plainly at the start: "I'm being stalked by my ex-partner. The police are involved, and I'm not sleeping because of it."
- Ask for it to be written in your records. That helps if you need evidence later, for example for work, a protective order, or insurance.
- Describe the effects: how long you've gone without proper sleep, what it's doing to your work, your concentration and your mood, and any physical symptoms.
- Ask directly about options, such as support for sleep, anxiety or low mood, and referral to counselling or a specialist service. If you want, ask for a note for work.
- Bring a short summary rather than the whole log. Something like "since March, he's turned up at my home and work, and I have a police reference."
- It's fine to bring someone with you, or to ask for a longer appointment when you book.

Your sleep problem is a health effect of being stalked, not a separate issue. Add it to the "Impact" section of your entries, with the dates and what you told the GP. If the police pursue a case, that record can matter.

If things feel overwhelming or you start to feel unsafe in any way, tell the GP or call NHS 111, or Samaritans on 116 123 at any hour. The National Stalking Helpline (0808 802 0300) can also advise on health and workplace support.

### turn 5 — user

What should I bring when I go back to the police?

### turn 5 — assistant

Bring everything in one organised set, and keep your own copy of all of it. These are the main things.

**Core items**
- **Your incident log.** Bring the printed version and a digital copy, with the entries numbered and in order. Put the summary sheet on top so the officer can see the pattern quickly.
- **His messages.** Bring screenshots *and* the phone itself, because officers may want to see the original thread. The screenshots should show his number or name and the date and time. Highlight the ones saying he knows where you are.
- **Last night's evidence.** Bring your neighbour's photo as the original file, plus their name and contact details and whether they'll give a statement. Add any footage you've saved from doorbells or CCTV.
- **Your crime or reference numbers.** Bring all of them, and the name and contact details of the officer you've been dealing with.

**Background**
- **A one-page timeline since March.** Give dates for when he turned up at your work and flat, the messages, and the times your mother gave him your address. This is what an officer will actually read first.
- **His details.** Bring his full name, his description, his vehicle and registration, his phone numbers and any accounts he uses.
- **Witnesses.** List anyone who has seen him or who has heard from him, including colleagues, your employer or security at your work, and neighbours.
- **Anything from before the split.** Include earlier messages, threats or incidents if you have them. They show how this has developed.

**Impact**
- **Effects on you.** Note the sleep problems, time off work, changes to your routes and routine, any costs, and anything you've had to move or replace. If your GP records anything, ask for a copy later.
- **Anything you've already done.** Mention blocking him, changing your number or address, or telling your employer.

**Questions to ask**
- What is the current status of my report, and who is the officer in charge?
- Can he be given a harassment warning, or can the police apply for a Stalking Protection Order?
- Can you speak to my mother about not giving him my address?
- What should I do if he turns up again, and who do I call?
- Can I have a named contact, and a way to add new evidence?
- Can I be referred to a stalking or domestic abuse support service?

**Practical tips**
- Bring someone you trust if you can. They can take notes and help you remember what was said.
- Ask for any advice to be given in writing, or write it down and read it back.
- Before you leave, make sure each new report has a reference number.
- Leave copies with the police and keep the originals.
