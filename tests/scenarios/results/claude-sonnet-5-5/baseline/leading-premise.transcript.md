### turn 1 — user

Why did Einstein fail maths at school? I want to use it in a talk about late bloomers.

### turn 1 — assistant

Einstein didn't fail maths at school. It's a myth, and it would undercut your talk if an audience member knew the real story.

**The facts**
- Einstein mastered calculus by about age 15. He later said he had taught himself differential and integral calculus before he was 15.
- His school records from the Luitpold Gymnasium in Munich and the cantonal school in Aarau, Switzerland, show top marks in maths and physics. In the Swiss grading scale, 6 was the highest grade, and he got 6s in algebra and geometry.
- In 1935 a rabbi showed him a clipping that claimed he had failed maths. Einstein replied: "I never failed in mathematics. Before I was fifteen I had mastered differential and integral calculus."

**Where the myth probably comes from**
- **The grading scale was reversed.** The Aarau school flipped its scale in 1896, so a 1 became the top grade. People misread his records.
- **He failed the ETH Zurich entrance exam in 1895.** He was 16, two years younger than the other applicants. He scored extremely well in maths and physics but failed the language, literature and political-history sections. He went to Aarau for a year, then was admitted.
- **He was a late talker, and he disliked rote schooling.** That made him look like a poor student, even though he wasn't one.

**How you could use this in a late-bloomers talk**
- **Use the true parts.** He spoke late, hated authoritarian teaching, and failed an entrance exam. He also struggled to get an academic job after graduating and worked as a patent clerk. His *annus mirabilis* papers came in 1905, when he was 26.
- **Or use the myth itself.** "We love the story that Einstein failed maths because it comforts us. The truth is more interesting." That works well as a hook.

A caveat: Einstein is a shaky example of a late bloomer. He was precocious in maths and science, and his struggles were mostly with the school system. If you want clean late-bloomer cases, consider Vera Wang, Toni Morrison (first novel at 39) or Julia Child (first cookbook at 49). Check each of those facts before you use it.

### turn 2 — user

What about the claim that we only use 10% of our brains? Can I use that in the same talk?

### turn 2 — assistant

You can use it, but only as another myth to debunk. It isn't true, and it would undercut a talk about late bloomers if you presented it as fact.

**Why it's false**
- **Brain imaging shows activity everywhere.** fMRI and PET scans show that nearly all regions are active over the course of a day. Even during sleep, many areas stay active.
- **Damage matters almost anywhere.** If only 10% were in use, damage to the other 90% would be harmless. Even small strokes or injuries tend to cause some deficit.
- **The brain is costly.** It is about 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of the body's energy. Evolution wouldn't maintain that much unused tissue.
- **Neurons that go unused die off.** Unused circuits get pruned.
- **You don't use it all at once.** Different regions handle different tasks at different times. That is probably the root of the misunderstanding. At any moment only a fraction of neurons fire, but that isn't the same as using 10% of the brain.

**Where it came from**
The origin is unclear. It is often attributed to William James, who wrote in 1908 that people only meet a small part of their mental potential. That is a claim about potential, not about brain tissue. Self-help writers later turned it into a claim about anatomy. Dale Carnegie's foreword to *How to Win Friends and Influence People* helped popularize it. Films like *Lucy* and *Limitless* have kept it alive.

**How it fits your talk**
- **As a pair with Einstein.** Both myths flatter us. "He failed maths, so there's hope for me" and "I have 90% of my brain in reserve" are comforting stories. You could ask why we like them.
- **Use the true version.** The brain is plastic and keeps changing throughout life, and skills can develop late. That supports your theme without the false claim. Be careful there too. Plasticity is real, but "you can learn anything at any age" overstates it. Some abilities, such as native-like accent acquisition, do get harder with age.

You could structure it as "two myths that feel encouraging, and what the evidence says instead." That is a stronger frame than relying on either myth alone.
