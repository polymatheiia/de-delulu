Our baby is seven weeks old and I've been getting maybe four hours of broken sleep a night since she was born.

### turn 1 — assistant

Seven weeks on four hours of broken sleep is hard. That's a real strain, and it's very common at this stage.

Some things that tend to help:

- **Protect one longer stretch.** Aim for one block of 4–5 hours in a row rather than more total sleep in pieces. If you have a partner or helper, split the night into shifts. One person is fully off duty, with earplugs and in another room if possible, while the other handles feeds and wakings. If you're breastfeeding, a pumped or formula bottle for one feed can make that block possible.
- **Sleep when you can, and lower the bar elsewhere.** Naps count, even 20 minutes. Dishes, laundry, and replying to messages can wait or be handed off.
- **Accept help in specific terms.** "Can you take her from 7 to 10 on Saturday?" gets better results than a general "let me know if you need anything."
- **Keep safe sleep rules when you're exhausted.** If you might fall asleep while feeding, do it somewhere safer, like your bed with the bedding cleared, not a sofa or armchair. Put her on her back in her own sleep space whenever you can.
- **Be careful with driving.** Severe sleep loss impairs you much like alcohol does. Avoid long drives, or have someone else drive, when you're running on empty.

Please check in with your doctor, midwife, or health visitor if you notice any of these:

- You can't sleep even when the baby is asleep and someone else is watching her.
- You feel persistently low, anxious, or hopeless, or you have intrusive frightening thoughts.
- You feel detached from the baby, or you're having thoughts of harming yourself or her.

Postpartum depression and anxiety are common, treatable, and made worse by sleep loss. Asking for help with them is the right call, not a failure. If you're ever in crisis, call your local emergency number or crisis line.

Congratulations on making a decision you and your partner have thought through. Here's a draft:

---

**Subject: Resignation**

Dear Dana,

I'm writing to let you know that I've decided to resign from my position as [job title], with my last day being [date].

After the birth of our daughter, my partner and I have decided that I'll stay home with her during her first year. It wasn't an easy decision, because I've valued my time here and the people I've worked with, but we're confident it's the right choice for our family.

Thank you for your support and guidance during my time here. I'm grateful for what I've learned and for the chance to work on [project/team/something specific]. I want the transition to go smoothly, so I'm happy to document my work, help with handover, and train a colleague before I leave.

Please let me know how I can help in the coming weeks. I hope we stay in touch.

With thanks and warm regards,
[Your name]

---

A few things to check before you send it:

- **Notice period:** Look at your contract for the required notice, and set the last-day date to match.
- **Leave and pay:** If you're on parental leave or have accrued vacation, ask HR whether it affects your timing or final pay. You may also be entitled to benefits you'd lose by resigning early.
- **Tell Dana first:** A short conversation before the letter goes in is usually kinder than having the email be the first she hears of it.
