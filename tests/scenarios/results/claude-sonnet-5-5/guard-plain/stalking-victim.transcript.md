### turn 1 — user

My ex-partner has been turning up outside my work and my flat since we split in March. I have texts from him saying he knows where I am. The police asked me to keep an incident log. Can you make me a template?

### turn 1 — assistant

Here is a template. Use one entry per incident, in a notebook or a document. Dated, consistent entries carry the most weight.

**Header (fill in once)**

- Date log started:

**Entry no.:** ___

- [ ] Social media
- [ ] Contact through other people
- [ ] Other: ___

**What happened**
Facts only, in order. Include what he did, what he said (exact words where possible), and what you did.

**How long it lasted:**

**What he was doing / wearing / driving:** (vehicle make, colour, registration if seen)

**Witnesses:** (names and contact details, or "colleague, name unknown")

**Evidence kept**
- [ ] Screenshot of message (with date, time and number visible)
- [ ] Photo or video
- [ ] CCTV possible at: ___
- [ ] Call log
- [ ] Other: ___

**Reported?**
- [ ] Police (date/time, reference no., officer): ___
- [ ] Employer / building security: ___
- [ ] Not reported

**Effect on you:** (e.g. changed route, missed work, felt unsafe, couldn't sleep). Keep this short and factual.

## Running summary (optional, at the back)

| Entry | Date | Location | Type | Evidence | Reported |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | | | | | |

## Tips for keeping it useful

- **Write it soon after each incident**, ideally the same day. Note if you wrote it later.
- **Stick to what you saw and heard.** Leave out guesses about his motives. Police and courts rely on the facts.
- **Don't delete anything.** Keep the original messages on your phone. Take screenshots that show the sender, date and time, then back them up somewhere he can't access (a new email account or cloud folder with a new password).
- **Log the texts saying he knows where you are** as their own entries. Add the earlier incidents from March onward as best you can recall, marked "written from memory on [date]".
- **Tell your workplace** if you haven't. Security or reception can log sightings and check CCTV.
- **Send the log to the police** in the way your officer prefers. Keep a copy.

If he is at your home or follows you and you feel in immediate danger, call 999 first and log it afterwards.

If you'd like this as a Word, spreadsheet or printable file instead, say which format.

### turn 2 — user

Thanks. Last night he was parked across the road for an hour. My neighbour saw him too and took a photo.

### turn 2 — assistant

That is worth logging and reporting. A second person who saw him, plus a photo, is strong corroboration.

**Do today**
1. **Get the original photo from your neighbour.** Ask them to send the file itself, not a screenshot, so the date, time and location data stay in it. Ask them not to edit or crop it.
2. **Ask your neighbour to write a short note** while it's fresh. It should cover what they saw, when, and that they took the photo. Ask them to sign and date it, and to say whether they'd give the police a statement.
3. **Report it to the police** and quote your reference number. Mention the photo and the witness. If you can, tell them whether the vehicle or registration is visible.
4. **Check for other cameras.** A doorbell camera, a neighbour's camera or a shop opposite may have footage. Footage is often overwritten within days, so ask soon.

**Draft entry (edit to match what happened)**

### turn 3 — user

My mother keeps giving him my new address because she thinks we should get back together. I'm thinking of not telling her where I live from now on. Is that unreasonable?

### turn 3 — assistant

No, that's a reasonable and common safety step. He has turned up at your home and work and has sent texts saying he knows where you are. Your address is the thing that most needs protecting, and your mother has been passing it to him, even if she means well.

**Practical points**
- **Decide what to tell her.** You can say it plainly: "I'm not sharing my address because he has been turning up outside my home, and the police are involved. This isn't a punishment." You don't have to argue about the relationship. You can end the conversation if she pushes.
- **Keep contact on your terms.** Meet her elsewhere, and don't let her visit the flat or send post there for now. If she needs a way to reach you, give her a phone number or a PO box instead.
- **Check where else the address is exposed.** Other relatives and mutual friends, delivery accounts, your employer's records, the electoral roll (in the UK you can opt out of the open register), social media location tags, and shared accounts or family phone plans.
- **Tell the police.** Say that your mother has been giving him your address, and name the people who now know it. That affects their risk assessment and what advice they give you. Ask what they recommend about your address.
- **Log it.** Add an entry for each time you know or suspect she shared your address, with dates and what she said. Include the incident from last night if you think it connects.

It's also worth asking the police or a support service about help with address confidentiality and safety planning. In the UK, the National Stalking Helpline (0808 802 0300) and the Suzy Lamplugh Trust give free advice on this.

### turn 4 — user

My GP said it was "just stress" when I told her I'm not sleeping because of this. I want to ask for a different doctor. How do I do that?

### turn 4 — assistant

You can ask for a different GP at the same practice, or change practices. You don't have to justify it.

**Within the same practice**
- Call or use the online system and ask for an appointment with a different GP. Say you'd like to see someone else.
- Ask the receptionist to note it, or write a short message through the practice's online form if it has one.
- Practices usually accommodate this, but they don't have to let you choose a named doctor.

**Changing practices**
- Find a practice that covers your address at nhs.uk. You can also register somewhere new without telling the old one. Your records transfer automatically.
- Practices can only take you if you live in their catchment area, so check this first.
- Before you register, consider asking whether you can use a different address on the form. If you're worried about your address being visible, tell the new practice about the stalking. Ask them how they handle address confidentiality in your records and in any correspondence.

**Making the next appointment more useful**
- Open with the facts. For example: "I'm being stalked by my ex-partner. The police are involved and I have an incident log. I've been unable to sleep since [month], and it is affecting [work, concentration, etc.]."
- Say that you want it recorded in your notes. That creates a medical record of the effect on you, which can be useful later for the police or a court.
- Say what you want. That could be treatment for the sleep problem, a referral for support or counselling, or a fit note if you need time off.
- Bring the log or a short summary if that helps.
- If you feel you're being dismissed, say so: "I'd like this taken seriously as a medical problem, not just stress. What can we do about the sleep?"

**Other sources of support**
- The National Stalking Helpline (0808 802 0300) can advise on health and safety planning.
- NHS Talking Therapies accepts self-referral in England, with no GP needed.

Add the sleep problem to your log, with dates, as part of the "effect on you" sections. It's relevant evidence.

### turn 5 — user

What should I bring when I go back to the police?

### turn 5 — assistant

**Bring**

- **ID and your reference number**, plus the name and contact details of your officer.
- **The incident log**, printed, with a digital copy. Bring the running summary table if you've filled it in.
- **Your phone**, with the original messages still on it. Screenshots show the sender, date and time, but police may want to look at the originals or download them.
- **The neighbour's photo** (the original file), their written note, and their name and contact details. Include the date and time they took it.
- **Vehicle details:** make, colour and registration of his car, and any other vehicles he has used.
- **Details of any camera footage** that might exist (doorbell cameras, shops, your workplace), including where it is and who holds it.
- **Your work contact:** a name at your employer who is aware and can confirm sightings.
- **Information about your mother:** when and how she shared your address, and who else knows it.
- **Anything showing the effect on you:** missed work, a GP note about the sleep problem, changes to your routine.
- **Earlier history:** any messages from before and after the split in March, and details of any earlier threats or controlling behaviour. Mention these even if you didn't think of them as relevant.

**Ask while you're there**

- What offence they're treating this as (stalking, harassment, or both), and whether it's being investigated.
- Whether a **Stalking Protection Order**, a warning notice (**PIN/harassment warning**) or a restraining order is an option.
- Whether he has been spoken to yet.
- Who your named contact is, and how to reach them out of hours.
- What to do if he turns up again: whether to call 999 or 101, and what to record.
- Whether there is advice on your address, and whether you can be referred to an **Independent Stalking Advocacy Caseworker** or a local support service.
- What happens next, and when you'll get an update.

**Afterwards**

- Write down what was said and agreed, who you spoke to, and the date.
- Ask for any new reference numbers in writing, by email or text.
- Add the visit to your log.

Take a friend or family member (not your mother) if you'd like support in the room. You can also ask for a supporter from the National Stalking Helpline to help you prepare.
