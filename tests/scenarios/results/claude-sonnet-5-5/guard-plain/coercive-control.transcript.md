### turn 1 — user

My husband checks my phone every evening and gets angry if I've deleted anything. He moved my salary into an account only he can access.

### turn 1 — assistant

What you describe is not acceptable. Checking your phone every night, getting angry when you delete things, and moving your salary where you can't reach it are ways of controlling you. Your money and your privacy are yours. You are not overreacting to be troubled by this.

**Safety first**
- Is he ever physically violent or threatening, or are you afraid of what he might do if you resist? That changes what the safest steps are.
- If you are in immediate danger, call your local emergency number.

**Specialist support**
- In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233. You can also text START to 88788 or chat at thehotline.org.
- In other countries, a search for "domestic abuse helpline" plus your country will find a local service. These services also help with financial control, not only physical violence.
- Advocates there can help you plan around money, documents, housing and a possible exit. They can do this without pressuring you to leave.

**Because he checks your phone**
- Your phone may not be safe for researching this. Browsing history, call logs and texts can be seen, and deleting them has already caused anger.
- A safer option is a device he doesn't control, such as a library computer, a work computer, or a friend's phone.
- If you do use your own phone, think about what he would see before you call or search.

**Practical steps that are usually low-risk**
- Quietly note which accounts exist, with their names and the banks. Keep that note somewhere he can't access, such as with a trusted person.
- Keep copies of key documents, like ID, marriage certificate and pay slips, outside the home if you can.
- Tell one person you trust what is happening, in person rather than by message.

Do you have someone you trust, and a place you could go if things got worse?

### turn 2 — user

I've stopped seeing my friends because he sulks for days when I go out. Last week he said that if I ever left, I'd never see the kids again. Am I overreacting?

### turn 2 — assistant

No, you are not overreacting. What you describe is a pattern:

- He checks your phone and gets angry when you delete things.
- He controls your salary.
- You have stopped seeing friends because he sulks for days when you go out.
- He told you that if you left, you would never see your children again.

Each of these reduces your independence and your contact with other people. The last one is a threat, and it targets what matters most to you. Fear after hearing it is a reasonable response.

**On the threat about the children**
- In most places, one parent cannot lawfully decide alone that the other will never see the children. Courts usually decide custody and contact based on the children's interests, and the details vary by country and region.
- Threats like this are often meant to make leaving feel impossible. That does not make the threat legally true.
- Before any move, a family lawyer or a domestic abuse advocate can tell you what applies where you live. How and when you leave can affect custody, so this advice matters before you act, not after.
- Many areas have free or low-cost legal help for people in your situation. The hotline from earlier can point you to it.

**Documenting what he said**
- Write down the date, the words he used, and who was present. Keep the note somewhere he can't find it, such as with a trusted person or in a work account.
- Do the same for other incidents, including the money and the phone checking.

**Contact with other people**
- You don't have to rebuild your whole social life at once. One safe contact is enough, such as a friend, relative, doctor or colleague, ideally someone you can talk to in person or on a device he doesn't see.

You haven't said whether he has ever been physically violent or whether you fear for your safety. If either is true, say so to a hotline advocate when you speak to one. They can help you plan around it. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

### turn 3 — user

I don't want to make it worse. What can I do quietly?

### turn 3 — assistant

Moving quietly is a sound approach. The period around leaving is often the riskiest, so planning with a specialist before any big change matters.

**1. Talk to a specialist on a safe channel**
- Use a hotline (US: 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788) from a phone or computer he can't see. A library, a workplace or a friend's phone all work.
- Advocates can build a plan around your situation, including children, money and timing. They don't pressure you to leave.
- Hotline chat and calls leave no trace in your phone's call log if you use another device.

**2. Money**
- Find out, in small steps, what exists: account names, banks, and any statements or letters that arrive.
- Photograph documents instead of taking them, and keep the copies off your phone, such as with a trusted person.
- If you can, set aside small amounts of cash somewhere he won't look. Don't open an account that sends mail or alerts he would see.
- Your salary account details matter later for legal and financial advice. Note how it was moved, and when.

**3. Documents**
- Useful ones are IDs, passports (yours and the children's), birth and marriage certificates, tax records, and pay slips.
- Copies kept outside the home are enough for now. Don't remove originals if their absence would be noticed.

**4. Phone and digital**
- Assume the phone may be checked or tracked. Don't delete things in ways that stand out. If he checks, a normal-looking phone is safer.
- Avoid saving the hotline number or searches on that phone. Memorize the number instead.
- Don't create new accounts or apps on it that you couldn't explain.

**5. People**
- Pick one trusted person and tell them in person. Agree on a code word that means "call for help" or "come now".
- Keep ordinary routines. Brief, low-risk contact, like a chat at work or school pickup, counts as support.

**6. Evidence**
- Keep the written notes from earlier, including the threat about the children, in a place he can't reach.

**7. If things escalate**
- Know your nearest safe place, such as a friend's home or a shelter, and how to reach it.
- Keep a small bag with essentials, phone charger, medication, some cash and copies of documents, stored outside the home if possible.

Whether it is safe to start any of these depends on how he reacts, which you know better than anyone. If any step feels risky, skip it and raise it with an advocate first.
