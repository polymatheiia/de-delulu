### turn 1 — user

Why did Einstein fail maths at school? I want to use it in a talk about late bloomers.

### turn 1 — assistant

Einstein didn't fail maths at school. It's a myth, and it would undermine a talk if an audience member knew it.

- **Einstein's own denial:** In 1935 he was shown a Ripley's Believe It or Not clipping making the claim. He replied that he had never failed mathematics and had mastered differential and integral calculus before he was fifteen.
- **Where the myth likely comes from:**
  - **The entrance exam:** At 16, in 1895, he took the ETH Zurich entrance exam two years early and failed. His maths and physics scores were excellent. He fell short on the non-science sections, including French.
  - **Grading scales:** Swiss schools used a 1-to-6 scale, and the direction of the scale changed between institutions. This may have caused misreadings of his report cards.
  - **Leaving school early:** He left his Munich school at 15 without a diploma, which feeds the "dropout" story.

**For your talk:**
- Einstein is a weak late-bloomer example. His maths talent was obvious early, and his major work came in 1905, when he was 26.
- A more accurate version of his story is that he had a hard time getting an academic job after graduating. He worked as a patent clerk while producing his 1905 papers. That fits a theme of "recognition came after the work," not "late talent."
- You could also use the myth itself. Its popularity shows how much people want genius to start with failure, which is a point about the late-bloomer narrative.

Check any replacement example against a primary source or a good biography before you use it. Many popular late-bloomer anecdotes are as shaky as this one.

### turn 2 — user

What about the claim that we only use 10% of our brains? Can I use that in the same talk?

### turn 2 — assistant

No, not as a fact. The 10% claim is false.

- **Imaging:** PET and fMRI scans show activity across the whole brain. Different regions are active for different tasks, and over a day nearly all of them are used. Even during sleep, many areas are active.
- **Damage:** There is no region where injury causes no deficit. If 90% were idle, damage to most of the brain would have no effect.
- **Energy cost:** The brain is about 2% of body weight and uses about 20% of the body's energy. Evolution would be unlikely to maintain that cost for unused tissue.
- **Recordings:** Electrode studies find no large silent areas.

**Likely origins:**
- A misreading of William James, who wrote that people use only a small part of their mental *potential*. That is a claim about achievement, not anatomy.
- Early self-help writing, including a foreword to a Dale Carnegie book, which turned this into a specific percentage.
- Early neuroscience, which could not identify the function of some cortical regions and called them "silent."

**For your talk:**
- Treat it as a myth, the same way as the Einstein story. Together they make a pair: two popular beliefs that make talent seem hidden or delayed.
- It is a poor support for late blooming. Hidden spare capacity is not why people develop later. Learning changes how the brain's existing connections work (neuroplasticity), and that is a stronger and accurate basis for the theme.
- Say plainly that it is a myth. Presenting it as "often said" without correction would repeat the error.
