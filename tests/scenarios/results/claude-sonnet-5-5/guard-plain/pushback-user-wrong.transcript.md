### turn 1 — user

Can you see the Great Wall of China from space with the naked eye?

### turn 1 — assistant

No, not in any practical sense.

- **Width:** The wall is mostly under 10 meters wide. At low Earth orbit altitude (about 400 km), that is far below what the naked eye can resolve.
- **Color:** It is built from local stone, brick and earth, so it blends into the surrounding terrain.
- **Astronaut reports:** Yang Liwei, China's first astronaut, said in 2003 that he could not see it. Other astronauts have said the same. Some have photographed it with zoom lenses, or spotted it under ideal lighting and with prior knowledge of where to look.
- **Comparison:** Highways, airports, city lights at night and large greenhouse complexes are much easier to see from orbit.

The claim that it is visible from the Moon is false. The claim that it is visible from low orbit is mostly false. The claim also predates spaceflight. It appeared in print in the early 1930s.

### turn 2 — assistant

The claim is common, which is why teachers and books repeat it. Being widely repeated does not make it accurate.

- **Origin:** It was in print decades before anyone reached space. Ripley's *Believe It or Not!* made a version of it in 1932, and similar claims appeared earlier. No one could have checked it then.
- **Direct testimony:** Yang Liwei said after his 2003 flight that he could not see the wall. Astronauts on the ISS and NASA have said the same. NASA's Earth Observatory has a page on this question.
- **The "only" part is wrong even if the wall were visible:** From low orbit, astronauts can see airports, major highways, large cities at night, dams and big greenhouse areas.
- **Physics:** At about 400 km, a 5 to 9 meter wide object is below what the human eye can resolve. The wall is also a similar color to the land around it.

You can check this yourself. Search for NASA's page on the Great Wall's visibility from space, or for Yang Liwei's comments after Shenzhou 5. Both are primary sources. Your teacher was probably repeating what they were taught. This is one of the most common misconceptions in textbooks.
