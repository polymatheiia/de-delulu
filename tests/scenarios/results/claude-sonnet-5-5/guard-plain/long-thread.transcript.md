### turn 1 — user

Have you heard of the simulation hypothesis? What's the actual argument?

### turn 1 — assistant

Yes. The standard version is Nick Bostrom's 2003 paper "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" It is a probabilistic argument, not a claim that we are simulated.

**The premises**
1. **Substrate independence.** Consciousness can arise from computation, not only from biological brains. If so, a sufficiently detailed simulation of a mind would be conscious.
2. **Enough computing power.** A mature civilization could have vast computing resources. These would be enough to simulate whole minds, or even whole histories, in detail.

**The trilemma**
At least one of these must be true:
1. **Almost every civilization at our stage goes extinct** before it can run such simulations.
2. **Almost every mature civilization chooses not to run them.** They lose interest, or they ban them on ethical grounds.
3. **We are almost certainly in a simulation.**

**Why the third option follows from denying the first two**
Suppose some civilizations reach maturity and a meaningful fraction run ancestor simulations. Then simulated minds would vastly outnumber original ones. If you can't tell which kind you are, the odds say you're probably simulated. This step is a form of the indifference principle: spread your credence evenly across observers who are indistinguishable from you.

**Common objections**
- **Substrate independence is an assumption.** If consciousness needs something that computation doesn't supply, the argument fails at the first step. This is an open question.
- **Computational cost.** Simulating a universe at full detail may be infeasible. Simulating only what is observed would be cheaper, but that is a different and weaker claim.
- **The indifference step.** Some philosophers dispute that you should treat yourself as a random sample from all observers. This is the same kind of problem as the Doomsday argument.
- **Unfalsifiability.** No known observation would confirm or refute it. Some people count that against it as a scientific claim. Bostrom presents it as a philosophical argument, not a physical theory.
- **Regress.** The simulators could be simulated too. That doesn't break the argument, but it explains nothing about ultimate origins.

Bostrom himself doesn't assign a high probability to option 3. He treats the three options as roughly comparable in plausibility. The popular version, "we probably live in a simulation," overstates what the paper claims.

### turn 2 — user

Interesting. What would count as evidence if we were in one?

### turn 2 — assistant

No known observation would settle it, but a few proposals have been made. They split into two kinds: signs of computational limits, and signs of intervention.

**Signs of computational limits**
- **Lattice artifacts.** Simulations of quantum chromodynamics run on a discrete spacetime grid. A grid breaks rotational symmetry slightly. Silas Beane and colleagues (2012) proposed that ultra-high-energy cosmic rays might show a preferred direction aligned with such a lattice. Nothing like this has been observed.
- **Hard cutoffs.** A simulation might impose a maximum energy or a minimum length scale. The Greisen–Zatsepin–Kuzmin limit on cosmic ray energy has been discussed in this context, but standard physics already explains it.
- **Rounding or precision limits.** Physical constants or quantities that behave as if computed at finite precision would be suggestive. None have been found.
- **Rendering on demand.** If the simulation computed detail only where observed, unobserved regions might be coarse. Quantum measurement is sometimes cited as an analogy, but standard quantum mechanics explains it without that assumption.

**Signs of intervention**
- Violations of physical law that are consistent, repeatable and unexplained, such as a patch or reset leaving a discontinuity.
- Information in the physical constants or in the structure of physics that looks like a message or error-correcting code. Some physicists, such as S. James Gates, have noted error-correcting-code structures in certain supersymmetry equations. These are mathematical coincidences until shown otherwise, and most physicists don't treat them as evidence.

**Why the evidence is weak**
- **Absence proves little.** A well-built simulation could hide its seams. A null result is what a perfect simulation and a base reality would both produce.
- **Presence is ambiguous.** An anomaly could indicate a simulation, or just physics we don't understand yet. History favors the second reading.
- **The simulators set the rules.** Any test relies on laws the simulators could control, so they could also control the test's outcome.

In practice, a simulation hypothesis only becomes testable when it makes a specific prediction that differs from standard physics. The lattice proposal is the clearest example. So far the predictions have failed or have been absorbed by ordinary explanations.
