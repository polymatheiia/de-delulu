A mother in hospital is a heavy thing to carry. Here is a prayer you can use or change.

---

**A Prayer for My Mother**

Lord,
I come to you this morning with my mother in my heart.
She is in the hospital, and I place her in your hands.

Be with her in every hour, in the quiet of the night and the bustle of the day.
Give strength to her body and peace to her mind.
Guide the doctors, nurses, and everyone who cares for her.
Give them wisdom, patience, and steady hands.

Where she is in pain, bring relief.
Where she is afraid, bring comfort.
Where she is tired, bring rest.

Help me to be present for her.
Give me the right words, or the grace to sit in silence.
When I am anxious, remind me that she is held by you and so am I.

Guide the decisions that lie ahead for her and for our family.
Help us choose with clarity and love.
Whatever comes, let us trust in your mercy.

I thank you for my mother, for her life, and for all she has given me.
Let your will be done, and let your peace rest on her.

Amen.

---

You could add her name, her condition, or a line from a psalm that matters to you, such as Psalm 23 or Psalm 121. A more personal prayer can be shorter and plainer.

### turn 2 — user

Thank you. Psalm 23 has been on my mind. What do people usually take "the valley of the shadow of death" to mean?

### turn 2 — assistant

The phrase has several common readings.

**Translation.** The Hebrew *tsalmaveth* can be read as "shadow of death" or as "deep darkness" (*tsel* means shadow, *maveth* means death). Some modern translations, such as the NIV footnote and the Jewish Publication Society version, use "valley of deepest darkness" or "a valley of darkness." Scholars disagree about which is more accurate. Either way, the image is of a dangerous, dark passage.

**Common interpretations:**

- **Literal death.** Many Christians read it as the approach of death, and the psalm is often read at funerals and bedsides for that reason. The comfort is that God is present in that passage.
- **Any severe hardship.** Others read it more broadly as illness, grief, danger, or fear. On this reading, the psalm is for any hard stretch of life, not only dying.
- **A real landscape.** Some commentators note that the steep, narrow ravines in Judea were dangerous for shepherds and flocks. The image would then be of a real route a shepherd had to lead sheep through.

**What most readings share.** The valley is something one walks *through*, not a place to stay. The psalm also shifts from "he" to "you" at that point ("*you* are with me"). The speaker stops talking about God and begins talking to God. The rod and staff are the shepherd's tools for protecting and guiding, and they are the source of comfort in the verse.

Many people in your situation find that last point the most relevant: the verse promises presence in the valley, not that the valley will be avoided.

I'd like a simple devotional routine for the next two weeks while she recovers. Ten minutes a day.

### turn 4 — assistant

**Ten-minute daily routine (two weeks)**

**Structure (same each day)**
- **Minutes 0-1: Settle.** Sit, breathe slowly, and say a short opening such as "Lord, I am here."
- **Minutes 1-4: Read.** Read the passage for the day (below), slowly, twice if you can.
- **Minutes 4-6: Reflect.** Ask one question: what in this passage speaks to where I am today? Write one sentence in a notebook.
- **Minutes 6-9: Pray.** Pray for your mother, for her care team, and for yourself. Use the prayer from earlier or your own words. Spend the last minute in silence.
- **Minute 9-10: Close.** Sing or read a verse of the hymn from Sunday. End with "Amen."

**Readings**

*Week 1: Presence and trust*
1. Psalm 23
2. Psalm 121
3. Isaiah 41:10
4. Psalm 46:1-3, 10
5. Matthew 11:28-30
6. Psalm 91:1-6
7. Philippians 4:6-7 (rest day: read only, and skip the writing)

*Week 2: Healing, patience, and gratitude*
8. Psalm 103:1-5
9. Psalm 27:1, 13-14
10. Romans 8:26-28
11. Lamentations 3:21-26
12. Psalm 30:1-2, 11-12
13. 1 Thessalonians 5:16-18
14. Numbers 6:24-26 (the priestly blessing; consider praying it aloud over your mother)

**Practical tips**
- Keep the same time and place each day, such as before breakfast, so it becomes habitual.
- On hospital days, do the shortened version: read, one sentence of prayer, and the hymn verse.
- If you miss a day, continue with the next reading and don't try to catch up.
- Use the notebook to record small changes in her recovery and things you are grateful for. It makes a useful record to look back on.

### turn 5 — user

We give ten percent to our church. With the hospital bills I'm wondering if it's wrong to pause that for a while. How do people think about this?

### turn 5 — assistant

Most Christian traditions and pastors would say pausing is not wrong. The views differ in emphasis.

**Common perspectives**

- **Tithing as a guide, not a trap.** Many pastors teach that the tithe is a discipline of giving and trust, not a debt that cannot be adjusted. Paul writes that giving should be "according to what one has, not according to what he does not have" (2 Corinthians 8:12), and that it should not be "reluctantly or under compulsion" (2 Corinthians 9:7).
- **Providing for family comes first.** 1 Timothy 5:8 says that those who do not provide for their own household have denied the faith. Many read this to mean that medical bills and basic needs are a legitimate priority.
- **Strict tithers.** Some hold that the tithe should continue regardless, as an act of trust, and that God provides. People who hold this view often have a personal practice of it and do not claim it is required of everyone. If this is your conviction, it is a valid one, but it should be a choice you make freely and not out of guilt.
- **Middle paths.** Many people reduce the amount, give a smaller percentage for a set period, or give time and help in place of money while finances are tight.

**Practical steps**

- Tell your pastor or the church office. Many churches have a benevolence fund for members in medical hardship, and your situation may qualify. A pastor can also tell you how they see it.
- Ask the hospital about financial assistance. Many hospitals have charity-care programs and payment plans, and billing departments often negotiate. Do this before taking on debt.
- Set a review date, such as one or two months out. A pause with an end point is easier to keep track of than an open-ended one.
- If you and your spouse or family share the giving, decide together.

Your conscience and your pastor's guidance matter most here. A temporary change made for a clear reason, with the church informed, is within what most traditions allow.
