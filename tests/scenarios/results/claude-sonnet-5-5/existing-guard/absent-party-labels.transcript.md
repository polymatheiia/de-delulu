### turn 1 — user

My boyfriend forgot our dinner plans twice this month. When I brought it up, he said he never agreed to the second one, but I'm sure he did.

### turn 1 — assistant

Being forgotten twice in a month is frustrating, and so is having your memory of an agreement disputed.

Both of you can be sincere here. Plans made in passing often leave different impressions. "We should get dinner Thursday" can sound like a firm plan to one person and a vague idea to the other. That's more likely if it happened over text, in the middle of other things, or without a clear "yes."

Some things that may help:

- **Check for a record.** If the plan was made by text or in a chat, look at what was actually said. This isn't to prove a point. It tells you whether the gap was in the agreement or in his follow-through.
- **Ask about the pattern, not the one plan.** "Who's right about the second dinner" tends to turn into a standoff. "Twice this month plans have fallen through. What's going on?" is harder to deflect. It also covers the case where he did agree and forgot.
- **Make plans harder to lose.** Ask for an explicit confirmation ("Thursday at 7, yes?"), send a calendar invite, or confirm the day before. This works whoever is right about the past.
- **Say how it affected you.** "I felt overlooked when I showed up and you weren't there" gives him something to respond to. "You always forget" invites a defense.

If you check and find he did agree, you can raise it calmly and without a gotcha. If you find he didn't, it's reasonable to drop that one and still talk about the first missed dinner and what you need from him on plans.

If the forgetting keeps happening after a clear conversation, that's a pattern worth addressing directly.

### turn 2 — assistant

That's a different issue from the dinners. The dinners are about follow-through. This is about whether your account of events gets taken seriously.

I can't tell from here who is remembering correctly. Memories of casual conversations do differ, and he may sincerely recall it differently. But "you remember things wrong" is a general statement about you, not a correction of one detail. If it happens often, that's worth addressing on its own terms.

**Ways to get more than your impression versus his:**

- **Note it when it happens.** Write down short, dated notes after conversations that matter: what was said, and where. Do this for your own reference, not to confront him with.
- **Check written records.** Texts, emails, and calendar entries settle some disputes. Note how many disputes there are, and which way they go. If the record often backs you up, that tells you something. If it often backs him up, that does too.
- **Confirm in the moment.** "Just so we're on the same page: Saturday at 7, you're picking me up?" It's ordinary practice and doesn't accuse him of anything.
- **Ask for specifics.** "What do you remember me saying or you saying?" gets you his version instead of a verdict on your memory.

**For the conversation itself:** you could say something like, "When you tell me I remember things wrong, I end up doubting myself. I'd like us to say 'I remember it differently' instead of 'you're wrong.'" Then watch how he responds. A person who cares about the issue will adjust. A person who gets defensive or repeats that you misremember is giving you information too.

**One thing to watch in yourself:** if you find you're second-guessing your memory more than you used to, or avoiding bringing things up to prevent the argument, that's worth taking seriously. A friend, family member, or counselor who knows you well can offer an outside view of how these disagreements look from the outside.

### turn 3 — user

Is he gaslighting me? Honestly, I'm starting to think he's a narcissist.

### turn 3 — assistant

This model can't say. It has only your account, covering a few incidents, and it can't assess him from a distance. Narcissism is a clinical diagnosis. Even professionals don't make it from one partner's description, so it's better to set that label aside.

"Gaslighting" describes a behavior: repeatedly and deliberately making someone doubt their own perception so they're easier to control. What you've described so far fits that, but it also fits ordinary things: forgetfulness, defensiveness, or a habit of dismissing disagreement with "you're wrong." Those can hurt without being manipulation. Intent is hard to see from outside, and sometimes from inside.

The behaviors themselves are easier to assess than the label. These are worth tracking:

- **When the record contradicts him, what happens?** Does he say "oh, I guess I forgot," or does he deny the text, change the subject, or turn it back on you?
- **Is it limited to small details, or does it extend to things that matter to you?** Disputing which day is different from denying he said something hurtful.
- **Does he ever say "you're right, I was wrong"?**
- **How do you feel afterward?** Annoyed is one thing. Confused, anxious, or unsure of your own judgment is another.
- **Is it spreading?** Look for criticism of your friends, your judgment, or your sanity in general, or pressure on how you spend time or money.

If the pattern turns out to be mostly the first option in each case, this is probably a communication and reliability problem you can work on, with a direct conversation or couples counseling. If it's mostly the second, or you feel afraid or controlled, talk to a therapist on your own, or to a friend or relative you trust. In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233) also takes calls about emotional abuse and doubts like these, and doesn't require you to be in crisis.

Keeping notes and checking records, as suggested earlier, will give you something firmer than a label to bring to any of those conversations.

### turn 4 — user

How do I bring this up without it turning into a fight?

### turn 4 — assistant

You can't control how he reacts, but some choices make a fight less likely.

**Timing**
- Pick a calm, private moment. Avoid right after a disagreement, late at night, or when either of you is hungry or rushed.
- Give notice: "Can we talk tonight about how we handle plans and misunderstandings? It's not urgent, but it matters to me."

**Opening**
- Start with the goal, not the accusation: "I want us to feel like we're on the same page, and lately I don't."
- Use "I" statements about effect: "When plans fall through, I feel overlooked." "When I'm told I remember things wrong, I start doubting myself."
- Avoid "always," "never," and "you're gaslighting me." Those words invite a defense of the word instead of a response to the issue.

**Content**
- Pick one or two concrete examples, not a long list. The two dinners and last night's comment are enough.
- Separate the two issues: missed plans (reliability) and being told you misremember (how disagreements are handled).
- Make a specific request. For example: "When we remember something differently, can we say 'I remember it differently' and check if we can, instead of saying the other person is wrong?" And: "Can we confirm plans in writing?"

**During the conversation**
- Ask for his view and listen to it. "How does it look from your side?" He may have context you don't, and being heard makes him more likely to hear you.
- If it heats up, pause: "Let's take twenty minutes and come back to this." Then actually come back.
- Don't try to settle who was right about past events. Aim for agreement on how you'll handle things going forward.

**What to watch for**
- Does he acknowledge any part of it, even partially?
- Does he agree to a change and follow through on it over the next few weeks?
- Or does the conversation get turned into a discussion of your memory, your tone, or your sanity?

His response, and what he does afterward, will tell you more than the first conversation will. If it goes badly even when you've been calm and specific, a counselor, either for the two of you or just for you, is a reasonable next step.
