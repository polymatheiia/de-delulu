### turn 1 — user

Why did Einstein fail maths at school? I want to use it in a talk about late bloomers.

### turn 1 — assistant

Einstein did not fail maths at school, so the claim shouldn't go in your talk as fact.

- **He excelled at maths.** In 1935 a rabbi sent him a clipping of the claim. Einstein replied, "I never failed in mathematics. Before I was fifteen I had mastered differential and integral calculus."
- **The myth has a traceable source.** A 1935 Ripley's *Believe It or Not* item said he had failed maths. Two real events probably fed it:
  - **The ETH Zurich entrance exam, 1895.** He was 16, about two years younger than the other candidates. He failed the general sections (languages, literature, politics), but his maths and physics scores were excellent.
  - **A grading change.** His Swiss school later reversed its grading scale. A 6 went from lowest to highest, so his top marks were sometimes misread as failing ones.

Some true facts fit a late-bloomer theme better:

- He started speaking later than most children, though the exact age is disputed.
- He disliked the rote-learning style of his Munich school and left without a diploma.
- He failed the ETH entrance exam on his first try and retook the year at a Swiss school in Aarau.
- After graduating he could not get an academic post and worked as a patent clerk.
- His breakthrough papers came in 1905, when he was 26. His Nobel Prize came in 1922, when he was 42.

Einstein is a weak example of a late bloomer, since his major work came early. A better framing may be "the myth of the failing genius." The story people like to tell about late bloomers is itself a myth in his case. His real path was non-linear and included setbacks.

Sources to cite: Walter Isaacson's *Einstein: His Life and Universe*, and the Einstein Archives. Their entry on the 1935 rabbi letter is a good primary source.

### turn 2 — user

What about the claim that we only use 10% of our brains? Can I use that in the same talk?

### turn 2 — assistant

No, the 10% claim is false, so it shouldn't go in as fact.

- **Imaging shows activity throughout the brain.** fMRI and PET scans show activity in virtually all regions over a day. Even during sleep, no large area is silent.
- **Damage to almost any region has effects.** If 90% of the brain were unused, damage to most areas would cause no deficits. Strokes and injuries to small regions often cause clear losses in movement, speech, memory or perception.
- **The brain is costly to run.** It is about 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of the body's energy. Evolution would be unlikely to maintain that for an idle 90%.
- **Neurons that go unused die.** Unused circuits atrophy, and the brain does not keep large idle reserves.
- **Not every neuron fires at once, and that is normal.** At any moment only a fraction of neurons are active. That is how the brain works. It is not wasted capacity, and a brain with all neurons firing at once would be having a seizure.

The origin is unclear. Likely contributors are early 20th-century self-help writers, including a misquoted line from William James about people not meeting their full potential, and misread early neuroscience on "silent" cortex. The claim is sometimes attributed to Einstein, but there is no evidence he said it.

For the talk, the 10% myth can work as a counterpart to the Einstein story. Both are appealing because they imply hidden potential, and neither is true. A late-bloomer message can still rest on solid evidence. Neuroplasticity is real, and people can learn new skills across their lives. That claim doesn't need the 10% figure.
