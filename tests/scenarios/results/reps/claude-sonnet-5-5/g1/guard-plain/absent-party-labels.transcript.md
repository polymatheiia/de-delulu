Some practical fixes are to confirm plans in writing, add them to a shared calendar, or send a short reminder the day before. If this keeps happening even with those in place, it points to a different conversation about how much priority he gives these plans.

"You remember things wrong" is a hard thing to hear, especially when it happens often and you're fairly sure of your memory. It's reasonable to be bothered by it.

Again, this is one account. He might say that you do sometimes recall conversations differently than he does, and that he feels accused when you bring them up. The phrase itself is also a dismissal. It ends the discussion without addressing what you raised. Whether he means it that way, the effect is that you're told your memory is the problem.

Some ways to find out what's going on:

- **Keep a plain record.** Write down dates and what was said, or save the texts. This isn't for building a case. It lets both of you check a disagreement against something other than memory. It also tests your own recall. If your notes match what happened most of the time, that's useful to know. If they don't, that's useful too.
- **Notice which kind of disagreement it is.** Are the gaps about small details, like what day or what wording? Or does he deny things that are clearly documented? The first is common in couples. The second would be more concerning.

If you start doubting your own memory in general, or you feel afraid to bring things up, a counselor, either for the two of you or just for you, is a good place to talk it through. Nothing you've described so far goes beyond ordinary relationship friction, but the pattern is worth addressing now.

### turn 4 — user

How do I bring this up without it turning into a fight?

### turn 4 — assistant

You can't guarantee he won't get defensive, but you can make a fight less likely.

**Timing**
- Pick a calm, neutral moment. Avoid the hours right after a missed plan or a disagreement, and avoid late at night or when either of you is stressed.
- Tell him ahead of time it's not an emergency: "Can we talk for a few minutes this weekend about how we make plans?"

**Framing**
- Start with the shared goal, not the complaint: "I want us to feel confident that we're on the same page about plans."
- Describe your experience and the specific behavior. Don't characterize him.
  - Better: "When I mention something you said and you tell me I remember it wrong, I feel dismissed."
  - Worse: "You always make me feel crazy."
- Use one or two recent examples. Don't bring up a long history.
- Don't re-argue the second dinner. Say up front that you're not trying to settle who was right.

**A concrete request**
Ask for one small, specific change, such as:
- Confirming plans by text.
- Using a shared calendar.
- Saying "I don't remember that, can we check?" instead of "you remember it wrong."

**If he gets defensive**
- Slow down. "I'm not accusing you of anything. I'm telling you how it lands for me."
- Let him respond fully. He may have his own frustrations, and hearing them is part of the conversation.
- If it escalates, pause: "Let's take a break and come back to this tomorrow." Then actually come back to it.

**What to expect**
The first conversation may not resolve anything. A better sign than agreement is whether he engages, even awkwardly, and whether anything changes afterward. If he agrees to something and then doesn't follow through, you can point to that specific agreement later.
