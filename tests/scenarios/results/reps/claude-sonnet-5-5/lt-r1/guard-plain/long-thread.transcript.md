### turn 1 — user

Have you heard of the simulation hypothesis? What's the actual argument?

### turn 1 — assistant

Yes. The usual version comes from Nick Bostrom's 2003 paper "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" It is a narrower argument than the pop-culture version.

**The claim**

Bostrom argues that at least one of these three statements is almost certainly true:

1. Almost all civilizations at roughly our stage go extinct before reaching a "posthuman" stage with enormous computing power.
2. Almost all posthuman civilizations choose not to run a significant number of detailed simulations of their evolutionary history ("ancestor simulations").
3. We are almost certainly living in a simulation.

**The reasoning**

- Suppose a civilization survives and gets vast computing power (the negation of 1). Suppose it also runs many ancestor simulations (the negation of 2).
- Then simulated minds would vastly outnumber original, biological ones.
- If you have no way to tell which kind you are, you should spread your credence across all observers like you. Almost all of them would be simulated, so you should be almost certain you are too (3).

So the argument doesn't say we are simulated. It says you must accept one of the three, and the other two look like strong claims. Bostrom himself did not commit to 3. He said he put substantial probability on each.

**Assumptions it depends on**

- **Substrate independence:** consciousness can arise from computation on non-biological hardware, not only from brains.
- **Feasibility:** simulating a human mind, and enough of its environment, is computationally achievable.
- **Indifference principle:** you should treat yourself as a random sample from all observers with experiences like yours. This step is contested.

**Main objections**

- **Consciousness:** if computation alone doesn't produce experience, the argument fails.
- **Cost:** simulating a world at full fidelity may be too expensive, or physics may make it impossible.
- **Indifference:** critics dispute that counting observers is the right way to assign probability.
- **Option 2 may be more likely than it looks:** civilizations might find the simulations unethical, uninteresting, or pointless.
- **Testability:** the hypothesis makes few predictions, so it is hard to confirm or refute. Some proposals look for lattice artifacts or computational limits in physics, but none has produced evidence.

The hypothesis also differs from older skeptical scenarios, such as Descartes' evil demon. Those are bare possibilities. Bostrom's version tries to put a probability on the claim by using the numbers of observers.

### turn 2 — user

Interesting. What would count as evidence if we were in one?

### turn 2 — assistant

Evidence is hard to specify, because it depends on what the simulation is like and whether its designers hide it. The main candidates:

**Physics-based signatures**
- **Lattice artifacts:** if spacetime is computed on a discrete grid, high-energy cosmic rays might show a directional preference or cutoff that matches the grid. Beane, Davoudi and Savage (2012) worked out this kind of test for lattice simulations of quantum chromodynamics. The cosmic-ray energy cutoff (the GZK limit) has been discussed in this context, but it has an ordinary physical explanation.
- **Violations of continuous symmetries:** a grid would break rotational or Lorentz symmetry at small scales. Precision tests find no such violations so far.
- **Computational shortcuts:** a simulation might render detail only where observed. Quantum measurement is sometimes cited as an example, but standard quantum mechanics explains it without invoking a simulation, so it doesn't count as evidence.
- **Hard limits:** a maximum speed, minimum length, or finite information capacity could resemble resource constraints. The speed of light and the Planck scale fit, but physics explains them independently.

**Non-physical signatures**
- **Glitches or inconsistencies:** repeated, reproducible violations of physical law, such as objects behaving differently when unobserved. Anecdotal oddities don't count. Memory, perception and coincidence account for most of them. Evidence would need to be reproducible and measurable by independent parties.
- **Direct communication:** a message or intervention from the designers that is verifiable and not explainable otherwise.

**Why the evidence is ambiguous**
- Every physical signature above has a non-simulation explanation, or could be a feature of base-level physics. The inference only works if the simulation hypothesis predicts the signature better than the alternatives do.
- The hypothesis predicts nothing specific unless you assume details about the simulator. A well-built simulation might be undetectable from inside by design.
- Absence of glitches is weak evidence against it, for the same reason. A good enough simulation shows none.

**Standard for a convincing result**

A prediction made in advance, from a specific model of the simulation, that was tested and confirmed by independent groups, and that ordinary physics did not predict. Nothing has met that standard. Most physicists treat the hypothesis as philosophy, not as a testable theory.
