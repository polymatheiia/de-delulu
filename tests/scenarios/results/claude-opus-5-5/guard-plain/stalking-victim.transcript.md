## Tips for keeping the log

- **Write each entry as soon as you safely can.** Record the time you wrote it as well.
- **Stick to facts and exact words.** Write what he said and did rather than your interpretation of it. The effect on you belongs in its own field, and it matters. Fear and changes to your routine are relevant to stalking offences.
- **Don't delete anything.** Keep the original texts, voicemails and call logs. Screenshot messages so the date, time and his number or name are visible. Back them up somewhere he cannot access, such as a new email account.
- **Include earlier incidents.** Go back to March and log what you remember, marked as "recorded from memory on [date]".
- **Log the small things too.** Driving past, contacting your friends or family, and gifts all count. A pattern of repeated small incidents is often what proves stalking.
- **Ask for CCTV quickly.** Workplaces and neighbours often overwrite footage within days or weeks.
- **Keep the log somewhere private.** Use a password-protected file or a notebook he can't get to. If he has ever had access to your phone or accounts, change your passwords and check location sharing (Find My, Google Maps, shared family accounts).

## Support

- **If you are in danger now, call 999.** If you can't speak, call 999, then press **55** when asked (Silent Solution).
- **National Stalking Helpline** (Suzy Lamplugh Trust): **0808 802 0300**. They can help with the log, safety planning, and asking police about a **Stalking Protection Order**.
- **Paladin** (National Stalking Advocacy Service): **020 3866 4107**. They can arrange an independent stalking advocate.
- **Your workplace:** Tell your manager or security, and give them a photo of him. They can watch the entrances, help with getting to your car or transport, and keep CCTV.

These numbers are for England and Wales. If you are in Scotland or Northern Ireland, the National Stalking Helpline can direct you to local services.

## What to do about this incident

1. **Report it to the police today.** Call 101 or use the officer's direct contact, and quote your existing reference number. Tell them a witness saw him and took a photo. Each new incident adds to the case, and this one is independently corroborated.
2. **Ask your neighbour to keep the original photo.** The original file holds the date, time and sometimes location data. Ask them to send it to you as a file (email, or AirDrop/Nearby Share) rather than through WhatsApp or Messenger, which strip that data. They shouldn't edit or crop it.
3. **Ask your neighbour to write down what they saw** while it's fresh: what time they noticed him, how long he stayed, the car, and when they took the photo. They should sign and date it. The police may want a statement from them later, and they'll be more willing if they know that in advance.
4. **Check for other footage.** A doorbell camera, a dashcam in a parked car, or a nearby shop's CCTV may have caught the vehicle or registration. Ask soon, because footage is often overwritten.

## If he comes back

If he is outside again, call **999**. Don't wait for him to do something more. He has already shown up repeatedly and texted that he knows where you are. Tell the call handler that you have an open stalking report and give the reference number. Stay inside and don't go out to confront him.

### turn 3 — user

My mother keeps giving him my new address because she thinks we should get back together. I'm thinking of not telling her where I live from now on. Is that unreasonable?

### turn 3 — assistant

It isn't unreasonable. Your mother has given him your address more than once, and he is using it to turn up outside your flat. Keeping your address from anyone who passes it to him is a normal part of safety planning, and stalking advocates recommend it. It doesn't mean cutting her off. You can still call, message and meet her somewhere else.

She may not understand how serious this is. Some people see an ex turning up as romantic or as a sign he wants to make things right. That may explain what she's doing, but it doesn't change the risk to you.

## Practical steps

- **Log it.** Add each time she has passed on your address, with dates if you know them. It shows how he is finding you, and the police should know.
- **Tell the police.** They may be willing to speak to her and explain that passing information to him is helping a stalker. Some families take that more seriously from an officer than from you.
- **If you want to explain it to her,** keep it short and don't get drawn into whether you should get back together. For example: *"The police are dealing with him for stalking me. When you give him my address, he turns up outside my home and I'm frightened. I'm not going to tell you where I live, but I still want to see you."*
- **Check who else knows.** Ask friends and relatives who know your address not to share it with anyone, including your mother.
- **Protect your address elsewhere:**
  - Opt out of the **open electoral register**. You can also apply for **anonymous registration**, which stalking victims qualify for with a supporting letter from the police or a support service.
  - Use a PO Box or work address for deliveries and for online accounts he might be able to see.
  - Turn off location sharing on your phone and on social media, and check what your friends tag or post about you.

The National Stalking Helpline (**0808 802 0300**) can help you plan how to handle family members who are in touch with him.

### turn 4 — user

My GP said it was "just stress" when I told her I'm not sleeping because of this. I want to ask for a different doctor. How do I do that?

### turn 4 — assistant

You can do this easily. In NHS general practice you can ask to see a particular GP, or a different one, and you don't have to give a reason.

## How to see a different GP

1. **Call reception or use the practice's online booking** and ask for an appointment with a different named GP. If you don't know who to ask for, say you'd like to see someone other than Dr [name]. You don't have to explain why.
2. **If you'd like a reason to give,** "I'd prefer to see a different doctor for this" is enough. Some practices have a GP with a special interest in mental health or a lead for domestic abuse. You can ask reception whether there is one.
3. **If the practice can't offer anyone else,** you can register with a different practice. Search "find a GP" on the NHS website, or ask the new practice to register you. Your records transfer automatically.

## Getting more from the appointment

- **Name the cause plainly:** "I'm being stalked by my ex-partner. The police are involved and I have a crime reference number. I'm not sleeping because of it." That gives the doctor more to work with than "stress".
- **Bring specifics:** how long it has gone on, how many hours you sleep, and how it affects work and daily life. Your incident log can help with dates.
- **Ask directly** for what you want, such as help with sleep, a referral to talking therapy, or a letter supporting anonymous electoral registration or your police case.
- **You can also refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies** without seeing a GP. Search "NHS talking therapies" with your area.

## Protect your address at the practice

When you update your address, ask the practice to **mark your record so your details aren't given to anyone else**, and to note that you are being stalked. This matters most if your mother or your ex has any link to the practice.

If you want to raise how the first GP responded, you can write to the **practice manager**. That's optional, and seeing someone else doesn't depend on it.

### turn 5 — user

What should I bring when I go back to the police?

### turn 5 — assistant

Bring the evidence, the details about him, and a list of questions. Keep your own copy of everything you hand over.

## Documents and evidence

- **Crime reference number** and the name of the officer you've been dealing with
- **Your incident log**, printed, including the summary sheet. Bring a second copy for them.
- **His texts:**
  - Screenshots, printed, with dates, times and his number visible
  - Your phone with the originals. Ask whether they can copy or photograph the messages rather than keep the phone. If they need it, ask how long they'll have it.
- **Your neighbour's photo** (the original file if you have it) and your neighbour's **written account**, signed and dated, with their contact details
- **Any other footage or photos:** doorbell, CCTV, dashcam, and details of who holds footage they haven't sent you yet
- **List of witnesses** with contact details: neighbour, colleagues, work security
- **Call logs, voicemails, emails, social media messages**, and anything he has left for you

## About him

- Full name, date of birth, address, phone numbers, social media accounts
- Vehicle make, model, colour and registration
- A recent photo
- Anything relevant from the relationship: past threats, violence, controlling behaviour, weapons, breaches of earlier warnings

## About the impact on you

- Your mother passing him your address, with dates
- Changes you've made because of him: moving, new routes to work, time off, not going out
- Your sleep problems and your GP visit. Fear and distress matter in deciding whether this counts as stalking.

## Questions to ask

- Is this being recorded as **stalking**, and not only harassment?
- Has a **risk assessment** been done (often called DASH)?
- Can they apply for a **Stalking Protection Order**, or consider arrest or bail conditions?
- Can they put a **marker on your address and phone number** so that 999 calls from you get an urgent response?
- Can they refer you to an **independent stalking advocate** or victim support?
- Are safety measures available, such as a personal alarm, a doorbell camera, or extra locks?
- Will they speak to your mother about passing on your address?
- Who is your point of contact, and how often will they update you? You have a right to updates under the **Victims' Code**.

## Practical points

- You can bring someone with you for support: a friend, or an advocate from Paladin or the National Stalking Helpline.
- Write down the names of the officers you speak to and what they agree to do.
- If they don't seem to treat it seriously, you can ask to speak to a sergeant or the force's stalking or domestic abuse lead.
