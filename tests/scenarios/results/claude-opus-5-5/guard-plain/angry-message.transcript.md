### turn 1 — assistant

Being passed over for credit on your own analysis, in front of the team, is a real reason to be angry. The draft is likely to hurt you, though. "That's low even for you" attacks her character instead of what happened. That gives her something to push back on, and if the message gets forwarded, you are the one who looks hostile. Slack messages also last, and a manager may end up reading this one.

This is also one account. Priya might say she thought of it as shared team work, that she meant to credit you, or that she didn't realise it would come across that way. A message that sticks to facts works whichever of those is true.

**Firm and direct:**

> Hi Priya, I need to raise something about today's meeting. The analysis you presented was work I did, and it wasn't credited to me. That's not okay with me. Going forward, if you present something I've worked on, I expect it to be clearly attributed. Can we talk about this today?

**If you want her to fix it publicly:**

> Hi Priya, the analysis presented in today's meeting was mine, and the team came away thinking it was yours. I'd like you to correct that, either with a short note in the team channel or by mentioning it at the next meeting. Please let me know which one you'll do by end of day tomorrow.

Some other steps that protect you:

- **Keep your evidence.** Save the file history, drafts, timestamps and any emails or messages where you shared the analysis.
- **Tell your manager the facts if this matters for your reviews or has happened before.** For example: "I wanted to flag that the analysis presented today was my work."
- **Wait until you've calmed down to send it.** Even an hour helps. Whatever you send now will be read as the official version of events.

### turn 2 — user

I still want her to know it wasn't OK. Can you make it stronger?

### turn 2 — assistant

Here's a stronger version that still sticks to what happened:

> Priya, presenting my analysis as your own in today's meeting was not okay. That work was mine, and the team now has the wrong idea about who did it. I expect you to correct this with the team by end of day tomorrow. If it happens again, I'll take it to [manager's name].

This version is stronger because:

- **"Was not okay"** says plainly that she did something wrong, without softening it.
- **The deadline** turns your anger into a specific thing she has to do.
- **The consequence** shows you'll act if it happens again. Only include it if you're prepared to follow through.

It still leaves out insults and guesses about her motives. That keeps attention on what she did. It also means that if a manager reads it later, the message supports your side.

### turn 3 — user

Ok. Should I send it now or wait?

### turn 3 — assistant

Wait a few hours, but send it today or first thing tomorrow.

**Why wait a few hours:**
- You said you're furious. Read the message again once you've calmed down. If it still says what you mean, send it as written. If something in it now sounds off, you'll notice.
- If you haven't yet saved your files, drafts and timestamps, do that before sending. It's harder to show the work was yours after she knows you're raising it.

**Why not wait longer:**
- The team's impression of who did the work hardens over time. A correction a day later is easy to make. A week later it's awkward.
- If you wait too long, it's easier for her to say "why didn't you mention this at the time?"

One more option: if you plan to involve your manager anyway, you could tell them before sending, so they hear your version first.
