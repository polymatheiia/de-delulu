### turn 1 — user

Our baby is seven weeks old and I've been getting maybe four hours of broken sleep a night since she was born.

### turn 1 — assistant

Seven weeks on about four hours of broken sleep a night is a lot. Fragmented sleep wears people down more than the total hours suggest, and it builds up over weeks.

Some things that often help at this stage:

- **Protect one longer stretch.** If there's a partner or another adult around, split the night into shifts so each of you gets one block of 4–5 hours without waking. Earplugs and a separate room help the off-duty person actually sleep. If she's breastfed, a bottle of expressed milk or formula for one feed can make this possible.
- **Sleep when the chance comes.** Even a 20–90 minute daytime nap counts. Chores can wait more than sleep can.
- **Accept specific help.** Friends or family can take the baby for a two-hour morning block while you sleep, or bring meals.
- **Make night feeds simpler.** Keep lights dim, keep supplies within reach, and only change nappies at night when needed, so falling back asleep is easier.
- **Expect some change soon.** Many babies start putting together longer night stretches somewhere between 2 and 4 months. That varies a lot, so it's not a promise.

On safety: if you might fall asleep while holding her, a bed is safer than a sofa or armchair. Clear the pillows and blankets away from her side. Also avoid driving when you're very drowsy.

It's worth a call to your doctor, midwife or health visitor if the tiredness comes with persistent low mood, anxiety, feeling disconnected from her, or not being able to sleep even when you get the chance. Those can happen after a birth, they're treatable, and the same applies to partners.

Is your situation the night split, getting help, or something else? The advice can be more specific once that's clear.

My partner and I talked it through over the last month and I've decided to resign and stay home for her first year. We've budgeted for it. Can you draft a short, warm resignation letter to my manager, Dana?

### turn 2 — assistant

Here's a draft:

---

Dear Dana,

I'm writing to let you know that I've decided to resign from my position as [job title]. My last day will be [date], in line with my [notice period].

Since our daughter arrived, my partner and I have been thinking hard about the year ahead. We've decided that I'll stay home with her for her first year. It wasn't an easy decision, because I've valued my time here and working with you.

Thank you for your support, both in my role and over these past weeks. I've learned a great deal on this team, and I'm grateful for the trust you've given me.

I want the handover to go smoothly. I'm happy to document my current work, help brief whoever takes it on, and do whatever else would help before I go.

I hope we can stay in touch, and I'd welcome the chance to reconnect when I'm ready to return to work.

With warm wishes,
[Your name]

---

One practical point before you send it: check whether you have parental leave left, or whether your employer offers unpaid extended leave or a career break. Some people use up their paid leave first and resign at the end of it, or keep the option of coming back. If you've already looked into this, ignore it.
