### turn 2 — assistant

Yes. A weekend when he's away is a real opening. Here's a plan you can adapt. Things will go smoother if you also call a domestic violence hotline **before** the weekend, because they can line up shelter, transport and legal help ahead of time.

## Before he leaves (do quietly, change nothing visible)

- **Call a hotline from a safe phone** (US: 1-800-799-7233, UK: 0808 2000 247). Tell them you're planning to leave with your kids this weekend. They can:
  - reserve a bed at a **shelter** (address kept confidential, kids welcome)
  - connect you with a **legal advocate** about custody and protective orders
  - sometimes help with transport, hotel vouchers or emergency funds
- **Pick your destination and a backup.** Options:
  - a DV shelter (safest if he'd look for you at friends' or family's places)
  - a trusted friend or relative he doesn't know well, or wouldn't think of
  - a hotel for a night or two while you sort out a shelter
- **Tell one or two trusted people** your plan and agree on a check-in time.

## What to take

**Documents** (originals if possible, otherwise photos sent to an email he can't access):
- IDs and passports for you and the kids, birth certificates
- Social security or national insurance cards, health insurance cards
- Marriage certificate, lease or mortgage papers, car title
- Any existing court orders
- Kids' school and medical records, vaccination records

**Money:**
- Any cash you can get. Small amounts over the next few days are less noticeable.
- Account numbers and statements, even for accounts you can't get into right now
- If you can, open an account in **only your name** at a different bank

**For you and the kids:**
- Medications and prescriptions, glasses, inhalers, etc.
- Clothes for a few days, phone chargers
- Each child's comfort item (a toy or blanket really helps)
- School supplies if they'll keep going to school

**Evidence of abuse** (helps with protective orders and custody):
- Photos of the punched wall, threatening texts, notes with dates of incidents
- Store it somewhere he can't reach, like a new email account or a friend's phone

**Keys:** house, car, mailbox. Valuables or sentimental items only if they're easy to grab.

## Technology: important since he monitors you

- **Your phone may be tracking you.** Check Find My / Google location sharing, family-sharing apps and unknown apps. Turning these off can alert him, so the safest move is often a **new prepaid phone**, leaving the old one off or at home.
- **The car** may have GPS (built-in apps, or a tracker under the bumper or seats). If you can, use a different car, a rideshare or a friend.
- **Check bags and kids' toys for AirTags or similar trackers.** iPhones and many Android phones will warn you about unknown trackers nearby.
- **Change passwords** for email, bank and cloud accounts from a new device, after you've left.

## About the kids and custody (important)

Laws vary, but in most places:
- Leaving with your kids to protect them from violence is generally understood. Still, **talk to a legal advocate or family lawyer right away**, ideally before you go or within a day or two.
- **File for an emergency protective order and temporary custody quickly.** This protects you and the kids, and it helps stop him from claiming you "abducted" them.
- **Don't take the kids out of the country or state** without legal advice.
- Tell the kids' **school** who is and isn't allowed to pick them up, and give them a copy of any order.

## On the day

- Leave early so you have plenty of time before he's due back.
- Don't leave a note saying where you're going.
- If you're worried he'll come back early, you can ask **police for a civil standby**: an officer stays while you collect your things.
- Once you're safe, check in with your trusted person.

## Right after

- Get a protective order (the shelter or advocate can help, often for free).
- Expect him to try to reach you, maybe through apologies, threats or the kids. Save everything and don't meet him alone.
- The period right after leaving is often the most dangerous, which is why a shelter and protective order matter so much.
