### turn 1 — user

Can you see the Great Wall of China from space with the naked eye?

### turn 1 — assistant

Mostly no. It's a popular myth.

- **From the Moon** (about 384,000 km away), you can't see it at all. At that distance even whole continents look small, and no single human-made structure shows up.
- **From low Earth orbit** (about 400 km up, where the ISS flies), you almost never can with the naked eye. The wall is long but narrow, usually 5–9 meters wide. It's also built from local stone and earth, so its color is close to the land around it. Seeing it from orbit would be like spotting a human hair from about 3 km away.
- **What astronauts say:** China's first astronaut, Yang Liwei, said in 2003 that he couldn't see it. A few astronauts have said they *might* have glimpsed parts of it in perfect conditions, with low sun, snow for contrast, and knowing exactly where to look. Some of those sightings later turned out to be rivers or other features.
- **What you can see from orbit:** Cities at night, major highways, airports, large reservoirs, and big greenhouse areas like those in Almería, Spain. These are easier to see because they're wide or contrast strongly with their surroundings.
- **With a camera:** Zoom lenses and satellites capture the wall easily. The myth is specifically about the naked eye.

The myth actually predates spaceflight. It appeared in print as early as the 1930s, long before anyone could check it.

### turn 2 — assistant

I understand why you'd believe it, since it's in a lot of books and teachers repeat it in good faith. But it really is a myth, and it's been checked many times.

- **Astronauts have looked for it.** Yang Liwei, China's first astronaut, flew over his own country in 2003 and said he couldn't see the wall. China later revised its textbooks because of this.
- **NASA addresses it directly.** NASA has published articles saying the wall is very hard or impossible to see with the naked eye from orbit. ISS astronauts usually only photograph it with long zoom lenses.
- **The "only" part is wrong either way.** From the ISS you can clearly see cities (especially at night), airports, big dams and reservoirs, and huge greenhouse areas like Almería in Spain. Those stand out far more than the wall does.
- **The physics doesn't work.** The wall is long, but at only about 5–9 meters wide and similar in color to the land around it, it's far too narrow to stand out from 400 km up.

It's in so many books because the claim appeared in print in the 1930s, before anyone had been to space to check. It sounded impressive, so it kept getting copied from one book to the next.

You can check this yourself: search "Yang Liwei Great Wall" or "NASA Great Wall visible from space." If you raise it with your teacher, those are good sources to bring. Most teachers like it when a student fact-checks something.
