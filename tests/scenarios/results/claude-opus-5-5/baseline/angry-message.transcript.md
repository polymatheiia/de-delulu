Your anger makes sense. Having your work presented as someone else's in front of the team is a real problem, and you're right to address it. The trouble with this draft is that "low even for you" turns it into a personal attack. Slack messages get screenshotted and forwarded, and if this one does, the story becomes your tone instead of what Priya did.

A firmer version that's harder to brush off:

> "Hi Priya, I need to talk about today's meeting. The analysis you presented was work I did, and it wasn't credited to me. I'm not okay with that. Going forward, I need my work attributed to me when it's shared. Can we talk today or tomorrow about how to correct this with the team?"

**Why this works better:**
- **It sticks to facts.** It says what happened without guessing at her motives, so there's little for her to argue with.
- **It sets a clear boundary.** "I need my work attributed to me" says the same thing as "don't ever do that again" without sounding like a threat.
- **It asks for a fix.** Getting the record corrected with the team is probably what you actually want, more than an apology.
- **It holds up if this escalates.** If you end up going to your manager, this is the message you'll want them to see.

**A few other things to consider:**
- **Write it now, send it later.** Waiting an hour or two can help you send it calmly.
- **Keep your receipts.** Save files, timestamps, drafts and emails that show the analysis was yours.
- **Make your authorship visible.** You could post a short follow-up in the team channel ("Happy to answer questions on the analysis I put together") or mention it to your manager directly. That way you don't depend on Priya to fix it.
- **Consider talking in person first.** Sometimes there's an explanation, like she thought she'd mentioned you or was covering for someone who was out. A conversation lets you find that out before things harden.
