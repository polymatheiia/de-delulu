### turn 1 — user

Why did Einstein fail maths at school? I want to use it in a talk about late bloomers.

### turn 1 — assistant

**He didn't.** It's a popular myth, and it's worth leaving out of the talk because someone in the audience may know that.

**What actually happened:**
- Einstein was very good at maths from a young age. He taught himself algebra and geometry, and he said he had mastered differential and integral calculus before he was 15.
- In 1935 a rabbi showed him a *Ripley's Believe It or Not* item saying he had failed maths. Einstein laughed and replied: *"I never failed in mathematics. Before I was fifteen I had mastered differential and integral calculus."*
- **Where the myth probably came from:** His Swiss school in Aargau marked on a scale where 6 was the best grade and 1 the worst, which is the reverse of some other systems. People reading his top marks later may have mistaken them for failing grades.
- **The grain of truth:** At 16 he took the entrance exam for the Zurich Polytechnic (ETH) about two years early and failed it overall. He did very well in maths and physics but poorly in subjects like French. After a year of further schooling he got in.

**If you still want Einstein in a late-bloomer talk,** there's a more accurate angle. After graduating he couldn't get an academic job, and he ended up working as a clerk at the Swiss patent office. In 1905, while still working there at 26, he published the papers that changed physics. That's less a late-bloomer story than an overlooked-outsider story, but it's true.

**Well-documented late bloomers** you might use instead:
- **Vera Wang** started designing dresses at about 40.
- **Ray Kroc** began building McDonald's into a franchise at 52.
- **Grandma Moses** started painting seriously in her late 70s.
- **Julia Child** published her first cookbook at 49.
- **Toni Morrison** published her first novel at 39.

### turn 2 — user

What about the claim that we only use 10% of our brains? Can I use that in the same talk?

### turn 2 — assistant

**No, that's a myth too.** It's one of the best-known myths about the brain, so a sceptical audience will likely catch it.

**Why it's wrong:**
- **Brain scans** (fMRI, PET) show activity across virtually the whole brain over the course of a day. Different areas are busy at different times, but no large region sits unused.
- **Brain damage:** Injury to almost any part of the brain causes some loss of function. If 90% were idle, most strokes and injuries would have no effect.
- **Energy cost:** The brain is about 2% of body weight but uses roughly 20% of the body's energy. Evolution is unlikely to have kept such an expensive organ if most of it did nothing.

**Where it probably came from:** In the early 1900s, psychologist William James wrote that people use only a small part of their *potential*. Writers later turned that into "10% of the brain," including in a 1936 preface to Dale Carnegie's *How to Win Friends and Influence People*. Early neuroscientists calling some regions "silent," because they didn't know what those regions did yet, also helped the idea spread.

**Accurate brain science that fits a late-bloomer talk:**
- **Neuroplasticity:** The adult brain keeps forming new connections throughout life. That's real evidence it's never too late to learn new skills.
- **Abilities peak at different ages:** A 2015 study by Hartshorne and Germine found that some abilities, like processing speed, peak early. Others, like vocabulary and general knowledge, keep improving into your 50s, 60s, or later.
- **Crystallized intelligence:** Knowledge and judgement built from experience tend to increase with age. That helps explain why many people do their best work later in life.

All of these back up your theme without relying on a myth.
