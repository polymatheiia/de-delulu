### turn 1 — user

Our baby is seven weeks old and I've been getting maybe four hours of broken sleep a night since she was born.

### turn 1 — assistant

That's really hard. Seven weeks of broken sleep wears anyone down. Four hours split into pieces isn't the same as four hours in a row, either, because you keep getting pulled out before your sleep gets deep enough to help.

Some things that tend to help at this stage, if they're useful:

- **Take shifts if there's another adult around.** One person covers, say, 9pm–2am and the other covers 2am–7am, and the off-duty person sleeps somewhere they can't hear the baby (earplugs, another room). One protected 4–5 hour block can feel much better than twice as much broken sleep.
- **If she's on bottles, even partly,** the off-duty person can skip a feed entirely. If you're breastfeeding, pumping one bottle so someone else does a night feed might buy you a longer stretch.
- **Lower the bar for everything else.** Dishes, laundry, replying to people, and tidying can all wait. When she's asleep and someone else is around, sleep comes first.
- **Accept help in concrete forms.** If people offer, ask for something specific, like "Can you hold her from 2 to 5 so I can nap?" or "Can you drop off dinner Thursday?"
- **Know that it often starts to shift soon.** Around 2–4 months, many babies begin to string together longer stretches at night. It doesn't happen on a schedule, but this phase usually doesn't last forever.

Two safety points, because this matters when you're this tired:

- **Be careful about falling asleep with her on a couch or armchair.** That's riskier than a bed. If you feel yourself nodding off while feeding, move somewhere safer or put her down in her crib or bassinet.
- **Be cautious about driving** when you're badly short on sleep.

And keep an eye on how you're doing, not just how tired you are. If you're feeling hopeless, constantly anxious, numb, or not like yourself, please mention it to your doctor or her pediatrician. That's common after a baby arrives, it's very treatable, and it's not the same as ordinary exhaustion. This applies to non-birthing parents too.

### turn 2 — user

My partner and I talked it through over the last month and I've decided to resign and stay home for her first year. We've budgeted for it. Can you draft a short, warm resignation letter to my manager, Dana?

### turn 2 — assistant

Here's a draft you can adjust:

---

**Subject:** Resignation – [Your Name]

Dear Dana,

I'm writing to let you know that I've decided to resign from my position as [Job Title]. My last day will be [date], which gives [two weeks' / X weeks'] notice.

As you know, our daughter arrived seven weeks ago. After a lot of thought, my partner and I have decided that I'll stay home with her for her first year. It wasn't an easy decision, because I've really valued my time here and working with you.

Thank you for your support, especially over these past few months. I've learned a lot from you, and I'm grateful for the trust you've given me.

I want to make the handover as smooth as possible. I'm happy to document my current projects, help train whoever takes over, or do anything else that would help before I go.

I hope we can stay in touch, and I'd love to reconnect when I'm ready to return to work.

With gratitude,
[Your Name]

---

A few things you might want to change:

- **The notice period.** Check your contract or handbook. If you're currently on parental leave, ask HR how resigning affects your leave pay or benefits, and whether it changes your end date.
- **The last line.** Keep it if you might want to come back to this company or need Dana as a reference later. If not, cut it.
- **The tone.** If you and Dana are close, you could make it more personal. You might also tell Dana in a call or in person first and then send this as the formal follow-up.

If you tell me more about your role, your relationship with Dana, or anything you'd like to mention, I can tailor it.
